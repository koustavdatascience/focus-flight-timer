# Project Waypoint Launch & Distribution Strategy

**Prepared for:** Project Waypoint  
**Launch objective:** Find the first people who genuinely enjoy the idea of turning a focus session into a small journey, learn from their behavior and feedback, and then expand distribution without turning the project into generic productivity spam.

> **Working positioning:** *Waypoint is a focus timer that makes a study session feel like going somewhere.* Choose an airport, set a route, and let a real map turn completed time into a quiet flight.

## 1. The launch principle: start narrow, then earn wider reach

Do not try to reach “everyone” on day one. A broad audience only becomes useful after a small set of real users can answer three questions positively: **Do I understand it? Do I enjoy using it? Would I return?** Waypoint's differentiator is not that it has a timer; it is the **visual ritual** of selecting a route and making focus feel tangible. The first launch message should show that ritual in a few seconds rather than describing a long feature list.

The initial aim is not downloads, followers, or a Product Hunt ranking. The aim is **20–30 people who try a full session**, give specific feedback, and reveal where the first-use experience is unclear. Public attention works far better after the product has a simple story, a reliable first journey, and a few recurring users.

| Audience | Why they may care | First message to test | Best first channel |
|---|---|---|---|
| University students and exam-prep learners | They already use Pomodoro techniques and often want a more motivating focus ritual. | “I built a focus timer where your study session becomes a flight on a real map.” | Friends, college groups, study Discord servers, study-oriented communities |
| Aesthetic productivity and planning enthusiasts | They respond to calm, visual tools and personal rituals, not merely feature lists. | “A quieter alternative to a plain countdown timer: pick a route and focus until you land.” | Short-form video, Instagram, Pinterest-style visuals, niche communities |
| Online co-study and accountability groups | The Co-Focus feature creates a natural reason to invite another person. | “Would your study group try a shared focus flight instead of another plain timer?” | Small Discord groups, virtual study rooms, direct outreach to group hosts |
| Indie makers and designers | They can give high-quality product feedback and may share the building story. | “I made a map-based focus timer because ordinary timers felt passive.” | Indie Hackers, maker communities, personal LinkedIn/X posts |

## 2. Prepare a small launch kit before sharing widely

Make these four assets once and reuse them everywhere. They should feel like a **personal project introduction**, not an advertisement.

| Asset | What to make | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 15–25 second screen recording | New York landing map → Find a place selects an origin → choose a destination → route appears → timer begins and aircraft moves. | Shows the concept immediately without requiring people to read. |
| One clear screenshot | The active flight view with map, route, aircraft, timer, and distance visible. | Gives posts a visual anchor and works as a social preview reference. |
| Founder note | A shortened, natural version of why you built Waypoint. | Gives early users a human reason to care and invite feedback. |
| One stable link | `https://project-waypoint-app.vercel.app/` | Every post, profile, and message should use the same public address. |

Do not invent reviews, usage numbers, or testimonials. Instead, ask early users one specific question: **“At what point, if any, did the flight idea stop feeling useful?”** That produces better product decisions than a generic “What do you think?”

## 3. A staged 30-day distribution plan

### Days 1–5: Instrument, invite, and observe

Begin with people who know you or share a genuine studying, design, travel, or building interest. Send 15 personal invitations, individually adapted to the person. Ask for a **10-minute try**, not a public post or a share. The first wave should be deliberately small so you can respond to every note and fix obvious friction quickly.

At the same time, create a simple tracking sheet with the date, source, whether the person started a timer, whether they completed a session, and their exact feedback. The website already has a feedback route; treat each response as product research rather than social proof.

### Days 6–10: Join communities as a member first

Participate in three to five communities where the project is relevant. Read each community's rules before posting, answer existing questions honestly, and do not repeat the same promotion everywhere. Reddit's own guidance emphasises that a person with a website is welcome, but a website acting like a Reddit account is not; it also cautions against vote requests and link-only participation.[1]

For a permitted feedback or self-promotion thread, publish the screen recording, state clearly that you built the project, explain the specific problem you were testing, and ask for a narrow form of feedback. If a community does not allow a launch post, do not try to work around that rule; ask a moderator first or leave it out.

### Days 11–15: Make one public maker launch

Once early testers can complete a journey without help, publish one reflective launch post on **Indie Hackers** and consider a **Product Hunt** launch. Indie Hackers offers founder, side-project, and product spaces that suit a transparent build story.[2] Frame the post around the design problem: ordinary timers felt passive, so you made focus visible through travel.

Product Hunt is appropriate when the product is usable to the public and you can be active in comments throughout launch day. Its guidance states that makers should launch as real people, that the product needs to be usable, and that paid hunting, spam, or soliciting upvotes can lead to removal.[3] Do not treat it as a one-click growth machine. Treat it as a concentrated day of conversations with early adopters.

### Days 16–30: Build repeatable, useful discovery

Publish one small piece of useful content each week, connected to a real user need rather than a keyword list. Examples include a short guide on setting up a 25-minute study ritual, a post about making remote co-study less sterile, or a behind-the-scenes explanation of why flight paths use real geography. Each item should link to Waypoint only where the connection is natural.

Begin inviting small creators and community hosts who already make study-with-me, focus, design, or travel-adjacent content. Send 10–20 carefully selected messages over two weeks. The offer is simply: **“Try this free project; if it fits your audience, I would value an honest reaction.”** Do not ask for endorsements before they have used it, and do not mass-message people.

## 4. Channel strategy: what to do, and what not to do

| Channel | Best use for Waypoint | Cadence | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct messages to known people | First real sessions and detailed feedback | 3–5 personal messages per day until the first 15 are sent | Group-blasting a generic launch message |
| Study communities and Discord servers | Reaching people who already value focused sessions | One rule-compliant post after genuine participation | Posting outside designated feedback or promotion threads |
| Reddit | Learning pain points and participating in relevant conversations | Comment regularly; post only when rules permit | Asking for upvotes, repeated link posts, or hiding that you are the maker [1] |
| Short-form video | Demonstrating the map-to-flight transition visually | One concise screen recording per week | Overproduced content that obscures the actual product |
| Indie Hackers | Build-in-public story and founder feedback | One launch story, then occasional honest progress updates | Treating it as a source of end-user student traffic [2] |
| Product Hunt | A focused public launch once the product is stable | One well-prepared launch day | Purchasing promotion, asking for upvotes, or launching before the product is ready [3] |
| Google Search | Long-term discovery from useful public pages and guides | Set up once; review monthly | Expecting immediate traffic after sitemap submission [4] |

You choose a route and the session becomes a little flight on a real map. Would you try one 10-minute session and tell me the first thing that feels confusing or unnecessary?

### Community feedback post

> I am building **Waypoint**, a focus timer where you pick an airport route and your timer becomes a flight on a real map. I am not looking for upvotes or promotion—just honest feedback from people who use Pomodoro sessions. Does the flight make a focus session feel more motivating, or is it distracting? ### Maker launch post

> I was tired of timers that only tell me what I have not done yet. So I built Waypoint: a small map-based focus timer where you choose a route and work until you land. The hardest part was keeping the map playful without making the focus session feel like a game. I would love feedback on the first-use flow and whether the travel metaphor earns its place.

## 6. Measure behavior, not vanity

Use a lightweight weekly review. At the beginning, manual notes and built-in site visitor counts are enough. The main question is whether people **experience the core loop** rather than merely open the homepage.

| Metric | Definition | First 30-day signal to seek | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qualified visitors | People from a specific community, post, or invitation | Identify which sources bring engaged people, not the largest raw number. | Where the message fits. |
| Route activation | A visitor chooses an origin and destination, then starts a timer. | Track the share of early testers who do this without help. | Whether the concept and controls are understandable. |
| First-session completion | A timer reaches its end. | Record completions from early invited testers. | Whether the ritual sustains focus rather than creating friction. |
| Return use | A person starts another session on a later day. | Look for a handful of unprompted returns. | Whether Waypoint has real habit potential. |
| Specific feedback | A message naming a concrete issue, use case, or delight. | Aim to collect 10 high-quality comments. | What to build or simplify next. |

Avoid announcing a numerical “success target” publicly. Early product work is better judged by whether you learn something concrete each week and apply it.

## 7. First-week checklist

| Day | Action | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Record the 15–25 second demo and write the personal invitation. | A clear explanation ready to reuse. |
| 2 | Send five personal invitations to genuinely relevant people. | The first feedback conversations begin. |
| 3 | Send five more invitations and review the feedback form responses. | Identify first-use confusion quickly. |
| 4 | Join three relevant communities; read their rules and participate without linking. | A credible, non-spammy presence. |
| 5 | Publish one transparent founder post where allowed. | One focused public experiment. |
| 6 | Fix the most repeated feedback point, then mention the change to testers. | People see that their feedback matters. |
| 7 | Review the tracker: which source led to real route activations and returns? | Decide the next week's single best channel. |

## 8. Search discovery setup

Your existing public sitemap and `robots.txt` are useful foundations. Verify the production URL in Google Search Console, then submit `https://project-waypoint-app.vercel.app/sitemap.xml`. Google describes a submitted sitemap as a hint rather than a guarantee, but Search Console lets you inspect processing issues and monitor queries, impressions, clicks, and indexing.[4] [5]

Do not chase generic “Pomodoro timer” rankings immediately; the category is crowded. Start with pages that reflect Waypoint's actual difference, such as **“map-based focus timer,” “virtual flight study timer,”** and guides that genuinely help a user create a focused study ritual. Publish only useful, original material that you would be comfortable sharing directly with a student.
